🇯🇵 How to plan & prep the perfect Japan trip for first timers

Japan is the easiest country in Asia to travel and the hardest one to plan, because almost every good thing has to be booked before you arrive. Here is the order I would do it in.

Start with the season, because everything else prices off it. Spring is sakura, but the bloom is only about two weeks and hotels go up months before it. Summer is festival season with the cheapest flights, and also 35 degrees and sticky. Autumn is the quietly correct answer: the best weather of the year and the leaves peak in late November. Winter is the cheapest, with powder snow up north and illuminations in every city.

For the route, book an open jaw. Fly into Kansai and out of Haneda so you are never doubling back. Two nights in Osaka, three in Kyoto, one night in a Hakone onsen, then four in Tokyo. Nara and Nagoya both work as day trips, so you do not need to move hotels for them.

I used to plan all of this in Google Docs and Sheets and it never worked, because most of my research came from TikTok videos I saved and then never opened again. Now I forward my tiktok links, travel documents and random notes into holicay.com and it saves the locations and organizes my notes for me in one place.

A few favourites for a first timer: Kuromon Market, Osaka Castle and Shinsekai in Osaka, Kiyomizudera, Nishiki Market and the Philosopher's Path in Kyoto, Nara Park and Kasuga Taisha in Nara, Lake Ashi and the Open Air Museum in Hakone, and Tsukiji Outer Market, Yanaka Ginza, Shimokitazawa and Golden Gai in Tokyo.

On accommodation, book six months out if you are coming for sakura or the autumn leaves. Kyoto sells out first and the same room can double in the peak weeks. Also know that Japanese hotel rooms are genuinely small, so pay for the location rather than the floor space.

Book the ticketed attractions before anything else. The Ghibli Museum in Mitaka is the strictest one: tickets are released at 10am Japan time on the 10th of the month, for the following month only, they cost 1,000 yen, and they are never sold at the door. Popular dates disappear in minutes, so set an alarm.

Language is less of a problem than people fear, but it shows up in odd places. A lot of ordering happens at a Japanese only ticket machine rather than with a waiter. Keep Google Translate and DeepL on your phone and camera translate the machine before you join the queue.

On trains, do the maths before you buy a rail pass. The seven day Japan Rail Pass is 50,000 yen and rises to 53,000 yen on 1 October 2026, while Tokyo to Kyoto to Osaka and back costs roughly 29,470 yen in individual tickets. On the classic route the pass loses. It only starts to pay off if you are adding somewhere far, like Hiroshima or Kyushu, inside the same week.

For data, get an eSIM before you fly. Ten gigabytes runs about 14 to 17 US dollars and covers maps, translation and messaging for a two week trip. Public wifi does technically exist, but most of it wants a signup form every single time.

Do these in order and Japan stops being overwhelming. Save this for when you start booking.
